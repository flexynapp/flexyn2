Training basics

Progressive overload, made simple

Your muscles adapt to what you ask of them. Ask for a little more over time and they keep adapting. That is the whole idea.

Updated September 2026

What progressive overload means

Progressive overload is gradually increasing the demand on a muscle so it has a reason to get stronger. Adding weight is the most common way, but it is not the only one. You can also add reps, add a set, slow the lowering phase, or shorten rest while keeping the same load.

The key word is gradually. A jump that is too big usually costs you reps or form, and the lift stops moving.

The double progression method

This is the easiest system to run without a coach, and it works for almost any lift.

  1. Pick a rep range, for example 6 to 8 reps.
  2. Use a weight you can lift for the bottom of the range on every set.
  3. Each session, try to add a rep to one or more sets.
  4. When every set reaches the top of the range, add weight and start again at the bottom.

How much weight to add

A common rule of thumb for barbell lifts:

  • Upper body (bench, overhead press, rows): 5 lb or 2.5 kg at a time.
  • Lower body (squat, deadlift): 5 to 10 lb or 2.5 to 5 kg at a time.
  • Dumbbells and machines: the smallest step your equipment allows. If that step is large, add reps first.

Beginners can often add weight every session for a while. Later it becomes every week, then every few weeks. Slower progress is normal, not a sign you are doing it wrong.

When a lift stalls

If you miss the bottom of your rep range two sessions in a row:

  • Check sleep, food and stress first. They explain more stalls than programming does.
  • Drop the weight about 10 percent and build back up with the same method.
  • Look at your recent weeks. If volume has climbed for a long time, a deload week may help more than pushing harder.

The part most people skip: writing it down

Overload only works if you know what you did last time. Without a log, most people repeat the same weights for months without noticing. Record the weight and reps of every working set, and look at them before your first set next session.
